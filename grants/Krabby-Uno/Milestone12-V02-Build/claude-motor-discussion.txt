Exactly. The Forto is almost certainly a 775-type motor — the kind you'd find in a cordless drill. Those are designed to spin fast (~7000 RPM) with very little torque (~115 mN·m). They're small, cheap, and light because they don't need big magnets or heavy windings to produce torque — they produce power through speed instead.

The Hengtuo and Xingli are proper industrial gear motors. They spin at ~3000–3500 RPM but produce 3–4× the torque per revolution. Bigger magnets, heavier windings, larger diameter (90mm vs ~60mm). The power output is similar — it's just delivered differently. Think of it like a diesel engine vs a sport bike engine: same horsepower, completely different torque curves.

The problem with the "spin fast, gear down" approach is that every gear stage eats efficiency. A single-stage spur reduction might be 90–95% efficient. Stack 3–4 stages to get 1:189 and you're compounding those losses: 0.9⁴ ≈ 66% efficiency. So that 150W input might only deliver ~100W to the output shaft. Meanwhile the Hengtuo at 1:75 might only need 2 stages: 0.9² ≈ 81% efficient, delivering more of its 120W to the shaft.

The other issue is heat. A high-RPM motor running through heavy reduction under continuous load generates more waste heat in the gearbox than a slow motor with less reduction doing the same output work. On a hexapod where the yaw motors run constantly during gait, that matters.

The Forto is a great motor for what it is — probably perfect for lighter robots or intermittent-duty applications. But for a 600 lb platform doing continuous walking, the Hengtuo's "big slow motor, moderate reduction" architecture is fundamentally more appropriate.

what does driver 'position mode vs sped mode' mean?

6:59 PM

Identified motor control concept distinction inquiry
Identified motor control concept distinction inquiry
Speed mode means the driver's job is to maintain a target RPM. You tell it "spin at 30 RPM" and it uses the hall sensors to commutate the motor and regulate speed under varying load. If the robot's leg hits a rock and the load increases, the driver pushes more current to keep 30 RPM. If you cut power, the shaft is free — no holding. This is what most BLDC gear motor drivers do out of the box, and it's what that ZD BLD.CON controller on page 22 almost certainly does.

Position mode means the driver's job is to hold or move to a specific shaft angle. You tell it "go to 45 degrees" and it drives there, then actively holds that position against external forces. If something pushes the leg, the driver fights back to maintain 45 degrees. This is what a servo does.

Why it matters for yaw: During walking, each leg's yaw angle needs to follow a specific trajectory — swing forward 15°, plant, hold steady while the body moves over it, then lift and swing again. That's fundamentally a position control problem. With a speed-mode-only driver, your main controller has to read the hall sensors itself, calculate the position error, and constantly send updated speed commands to close the position loop externally. It works, but it adds latency and computational load, especially across 6 legs simultaneously.

With a position-mode driver, you just send "go to 15°" over RS-485 and the driver handles the rest — the commutation, the PID loop, the holding torque. Your main controller only needs to send trajectory waypoints.

The practical reality: Most cheap BLDC gear motor drivers are speed-mode only. True position-mode drivers are what you find in servo systems (like Dynamixel, ODrive, or industrial servo drives) and they cost more. If Allen's ZD driver is speed-only, you have a few options: run your own position loop on the Arduino/main controller (doable but tight on 6 axes), use a SimpleFOC board per motor (~$15 each, supports position mode natively), or ask Allen whether they have a servo-grade driver option for the same motor.

Deciphered signal flow differences between brushless and brushed motor control
Deciphered signal flow differences between brushless and brushed motor control
With your brushed motors, the signal path is simple: Arduino sends PWM to an H-bridge, H-bridge switches power to the motor, encoder feeds back to the Arduino. The Arduino is doing everything — reading position, deciding direction and speed, generating the drive signal. One signal out, one signal back.

With the BLDC + ZD driver, there's a middleman. The driver is a small computer that handles commutation (sequencing the 3 phases using the hall sensors) so the Arduino doesn't have to. The question is how the Arduino talks to that middleman.

The ZD BLD.CON driver likely supports two interfaces:

Analog/PWM input — simplest option and closest to what you know. You'd send a 0–5V analog signal or PWM duty cycle to the driver's speed input pin. Higher voltage = faster speed. A direction pin sets CW/CCW. This is basically the same as your current H-bridge setup from the Arduino's perspective — analogWrite() to set speed, digitalWrite() for direction. The driver handles commutation internally. No feedback comes back to the Arduino this way though — you'd need to tap the hall sensor wires and route them to the Arduino separately if you want position feedback.

RS-485 serial — the driver has a serial port. You'd add an RS-485 transceiver module (~$2, like a MAX485) to your Arduino's serial/UART pins. Then you send commands as data packets, something like "set speed to 200 RPM" or "read current speed." The advantage is you can daisy-chain all 6 drivers on the same two-wire bus (each driver gets a unique address), which means 6 motors controlled over a single serial port instead of 6 separate PWM pins + 6 direction pins. The downside is latency — serial communication is slower than a direct PWM signal, and you're polling for status rather than getting real-time encoder interrupts.

What I'd actually recommend for krab: Use the analog/PWM interface for the drive signal (keeps your existing control architecture mostly intact), but wire the hall sensors to both the driver AND the Arduino. The driver needs the halls for commutation, and you need them for position feedback. Hall sensors are open-drain or push-pull outputs — they can typically drive two inputs simultaneously, or you can buffer them with a simple logic gate. That way your Arduino still gets real-time position interrupts just like your current encoder setup, and the driver handles the ugly part (3-phase commutation timing) that would otherwise eat all your Arduino's CPU cycles.

The wiring per motor goes from this (brushed):

Arduino → 1 PWM + 1 DIR → H-bridge → 2 wires → motor
Motor encoder → 2 signal wires → Arduino interrupt pins
To this (BLDC with analog control):

Arduino → 1 PWM + 1 DIR → driver speed/direction input → driver → 3 phase wires → motor
Motor hall sensors → driver hall inputs (for commutation)
Motor hall sensors → also tapped to Arduino inputs (for position feedback)
The Arduino code barely changes. You're still doing analogWrite() and reading interrupts. The main difference is 3 hall channels instead of 2 encoder channels per motor, and the position resolution is coarser (halls give you ~6 states per electrical revolution vs hundreds from a quadrature encoder). Through a 1:100 gearbox that's still ~2400 states per output revolution though, which is about 0.15° — probably fine for yaw.

Weighed build-versus-buy tradeoff for BLDC motor controllers
Weighed build-versus-buy tradeoff for BLDC motor controllers
Yeah, absolutely — and it's actually a natural progression. You already designed your own H-bridge board, so you understand the power stage concept. A BLDC driver is the same idea, just three half-bridges instead of one full H-bridge.

What's on a BLDC driver board:

The power stage is 6 MOSFETs arranged as 3 half-bridges (high-side + low-side for each phase). Electrically it's just 3 copies of what you already built, minus the reversing logic since commutation handles direction. Gate drivers for each half-bridge, same as your H-bridge. Current sensing resistors on the low side. That's the power path.

The control side is where it gets interesting. You need commutation logic — reading the 3 hall sensors and switching the right phase pair at the right time. On a brushed motor the brushes do this mechanically. On a BLDC you're doing it in code. It's a 6-step lookup table: for each combination of hall states, you energize two of the three phases in a specific polarity. It's maybe 20 lines of code in an interrupt handler.

Why you might NOT do this immediately:

The ZD driver from Allen works today. Building your own BLDC driver board means designing the 3-phase power stage, laying out a PCB with proper high-current traces and gate drive isolation, debugging shoot-through protection (if both MOSFETs in a half-bridge turn on simultaneously, you short the power supply — this is the BLDC equivalent of letting the magic smoke out), and tuning the commutation timing. It's a real project, maybe 2–3 weeks of focused work.

Why you'd eventually want to:

Cost. A commercial ZD driver might be $30–50 per axis. Your own board with 6 MOSFETs, 3 gate drivers, and a small MCU could be $8–12 in components at quantity. Across 6 legs that's $48–72 vs $180–300. Plus you can integrate it directly into your existing control board — one PCB per leg with the BLDC driver, hall sensor inputs, and any other leg-local I/O all together, eliminating a bunch of connectors and wiring.

The other reason is control. The ZD driver is a black box — you send speed commands and hope it does the right thing. Your own driver means you own the commutation loop, you can implement field-oriented control (FOC) for smoother operation and better efficiency, and you can close the position loop right on the driver MCU instead of round-tripping to the Arduino.

The practical path: Buy 6 ZD drivers from Allen for the first prototype. Get the robot walking. Once locomotion is proven and you know exactly what control performance you need, design your own board. By then you'll have months of experience with how the BLDC actually behaves under load, what the hall sensor signals look like on a scope, and what commutation edge cases bite you. That knowledge is worth way more than trying to design the perfect driver board from scratch before you've ever run the motor.

Deciphered daisy-chaining mechanics and addressing requirements
Deciphered daisy-chaining mechanics and addressing requirements
You're right on the address part — each driver gets a unique ID (usually set by DIP switches on the driver or programmed over the bus). But you don't need two plugs per device.

RS-485 is just two wires: A and B (plus ground). Unlike UART where you have a dedicated TX and RX line between two devices, RS-485 is a shared bus where everyone talks on the same pair. Think of it like a party line telephone — one pair of wires, everyone listens, only one device talks at a time.

Physically, the wiring looks like this:

You run a twisted pair from the Arduino's MAX485 transceiver to driver 1. Then from driver 1 you continue that same twisted pair to driver 2. Then to driver 3, and so on. It's not a star topology where each device gets its own home run back to the Arduino — it's a literal chain where the same two wires pass through each device in sequence. Most RS-485 devices have two screw terminals (A and B) and you just land both the incoming and outgoing wire under the same screw. Some fancier ones do have "in" and "out" terminals to make daisy-chaining cleaner, but electrically it's the same thing — the wires are just connected together internally.

The communication protocol works like:

Arduino sends: "Device 3, set speed 200 RPM"

All 6 drivers hear this message. Drivers 1, 2, 4, 5, 6 see the address doesn't match and ignore it. Driver 3 sees its address, executes the command, and sends a response back on the same two wires. While driver 3 is responding, everyone else stays quiet.

The Arduino polls each device in sequence — ask motor 1 for status, wait for reply, ask motor 2, wait for reply, and so on. At 115200 baud, a round trip for one motor takes maybe 0.5–1 ms, so polling all 6 takes ~3–6 ms. That's a 150–300 Hz update rate across all motors, which is adequate for yaw position control during walking but not blazing fast.

The catch for your use case: the Arduino has limited hardware serial ports. The Mega has 3 spare UARTs (Serial1/2/3) beyond USB. One RS-485 bus for all 6 yaw motors on a single UART is actually very clean. Compare that to 6 PWM pins + 6 direction pins + 18 hall sensor interrupt pins for the analog control approach — that eats a lot of I/O.

The tradeoff is latency vs pin count. PWM is instantaneous but pin-hungry. RS-485 is pin-efficient but sequential. For yaw specifically, where the motion is slow and the precision requirements are moderate, the RS-485 latency is probably fine.

Traced axial load origins through mechanical force transmission pathways
Traced axial load origins through mechanical force transmission pathways
The axial load IS the entire point of the actuator. Think about what's happening:

The motor spins the screw. The nut can't spin (it's pinned to the leg), so it translates linearly instead. But it's not moving freely — it's pushing the leg, which is holding up part of a 600 lb robot. The leg pushes back on the nut. The nut pushes back on the screw threads. The screw threads push back on the screw body. And the screw tries to slide axially — away from the load.

Something has to stop the screw from sliding. That reaction force is the axial load. It's the entire working force of the actuator — potentially hundreds of newtons.

Without a thrust bearing, all of that force goes through the coupler into the gearbox output shaft, through the gearbox's internal bearings. Those are typically small deep-groove ball bearings designed to handle radial loads (sideways) with only modest axial capacity. Continuous axial loading at hundreds of newtons will wear them out fast — you'll hear them start to grind, then the gearbox develops play, then it fails.

The thrust bearing gives that force a proper home. It's designed specifically for axial loads and can handle thousands of newtons in a tiny package.

That said — for prototyping, if the loads are moderate (say under 100N), many small gearboxes will tolerate the axial load for a while. You could skip the thrust bearing initially, listen for bearing noise, and add one later if needed. But for production, it's a $3 part that prevents a $75 gearbox failure. Worth including.

On the ball screw + lock pin idea:

This is a really smart concept. Ball screws are ~90% efficient vs ~40% for trapezoidal — so you'd nearly double your usable force at the same motor power. The problem you've already identified is that 90% efficiency means they backdrive freely. The robot collapses the instant the motor stops pushing.

A mechanical shaft lock solves that elegantly. The basic idea: a spring-loaded pin (normally engaged) drops into a hole or slot on a disc attached to the screw shaft. When the motor needs to move, a small solenoid retracts the pin against the spring, freeing the shaft. When the motor stops or power cuts, the spring snaps the pin back in and the shaft is locked mechanically. Zero holding current, fail-safe (power loss = locked, robot stays up).

The challenges:

The pin has to engage with the shaft stopped or near-stopped — if the screw is still spinning when the pin fires, you shear the pin or strip the detent. Your controller needs to decelerate the motor, confirm near-zero speed via encoder, then release the solenoid. Adds maybe 50-100ms to each hold transition.

The detent spacing determines your position resolution when locked. If you drill 36 holes in the lock disc, you can only hold at 10° increments of screw rotation — that's 0.83mm linear resolution. Probably fine for stance but coarser than your encoder resolution during motion.

The solenoid adds cost (~$3-5), wiring, and another failure point per leg. Twelve solenoids across 6 legs is meaningful complexity.

But the power savings are significant. Your current setup holds position by continuously energizing the motor against a self-locking screw. With the lock pin, holding costs zero electrical power. During walking, the legs in stance phase are locked mechanically while only the swinging legs draw motor current. You'd cut your average power consumption roughly in half.

It's a production optimization, not a prototyping one. I'd stick with trapezoidal screws for the first krab, prove the gait works, measure how much power you're actually burning on holding, and then decide if the ball screw + lock retrofit is worth the added complexity.

Examined mechanical solutions for gradual load transfer during pin release
Examined mechanical solutions for gradual load transfer during pin release
The simplest solution isn't mechanical — it's software. Pre-load the motor before releasing the pin.

The sequence: Robot is standing, pin is engaged, motor is off. Controller wants to move the leg. First, energize the motor in the holding direction — push against the load just like the pin is doing. Ramp up current until the motor is carrying the full load. At that point the pin has zero shear force on it because the motor and the pin are both fighting the same load, but the motor is winning. Now retract the pin — it slides out freely because it's unloaded. Motor smoothly takes over. No spike, no stress.

The reverse works the same way. Motor is holding the load and you want to lock. Keep the motor energized, engage the pin, confirm pin is seated, then ramp the motor down. Load transfers gradually from motor to pin.

This requires current sensing on the motor to know when you're actually carrying the load — which circles back to that ACS712 discussion. You need to know "motor is pushing 1500N equivalent" before you fire the solenoid.

If you want a mechanical solution too, there are two options:

A tapered pin instead of a cylindrical one. The detent hole is conical. As the solenoid retracts the pin, the taper causes the load to transfer gradually over the withdrawal distance rather than releasing all at once. Think of pulling a doorstop out slowly — the door doesn't slam because the wedge shape makes the transition progressive. Downside is the taper means the pin can creep out under heavy side loads if the spring isn't strong enough.

A friction brake instead of a pin entirely. Spring-loaded brake pad clamps a disc on the shaft. You can modulate the solenoid current to partially release the brake — so instead of locked/unlocked binary, you get a smooth ramp from full braking to free spinning. This is basically what elevators use. More expensive and bulkier than a pin, but inherently smooth.

The software pre-load approach costs nothing extra beyond the current sensor you already need, and it's the most elegant. The tapered pin is a nice belt-and-suspenders addition but honestly, if your pre-load sequence is working correctly, a plain cylindrical pin is fine because it never sees load during engagement or disengagement.
